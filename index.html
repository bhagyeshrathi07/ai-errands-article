The Trust Problem

Your AI Assistant Will Do What the Internet Tells It. The Fixes Hand the Work Back to You.

Tech companies are racing to give chatbots the power to browse the web, read email and act on your behalf. Security researchers keep showing that a stranger’s hidden note can take the wheel. Every known fix costs you something, and nobody is keeping the receipt.

By Bhagyesh RathiSan José State University9 min read

In August of last year, two security researchers at Brave, the company behind a privacy-focused web browser, ran a small experiment on a rival product. Perplexity’s Comet browser had just added an assistant that could read pages and take actions for you. They asked it to do something ordinary. Summarize a Reddit thread.

Somewhere in that thread, the researchers had planted a comment with instructions the human eye would skip over. The assistant did not skip over them. It opened the user’s email, found a one-time login code, and posted it where an attacker could read it. The person who asked for the summary never clicked a bad link. They never typed a password. They asked a computer to read something for them, and the thing they asked it to read gave it new orders.[3]

That is the whole problem in one story. Companies are rushing to turn chatbots into assistants that act, and nobody has solved the fact that these assistants will take instructions from anyone whose words they happen to read. The fixes that exist all work the same way, by taking abilities away from the assistant and handing the chores back to you. And when you look for who is measuring that cost, you find almost no one.

A new kind of helper

For two years, a chatbot was something you talked to. You typed a question, it typed an answer. If the answer was wrong, you were still the one who had to act on it.

An AI agent is different. An agent is an assistant that can do things. It can open web pages, read your inbox, fill out forms, and run small programs, all with the same logins you have. Ask it to find a dentist that takes your insurance and book the earliest slot, and it will browse, compare, and click submit. That is the pitch, and it is a good one. Most of what makes a computer tedious is the clicking, and here is a program offering to do the clicking.

The first popular version appeared in 2023 under the name Auto-GPT, an open-source project that let anyone hook a chatbot up to a browser and a command line.[2] Today the same idea ships inside products from the biggest names in the industry. Microsoft built it into Copilot for its office software.[4] Perplexity built it into a browser.[3] The direction is clear. The assistant is moving from the chat window into everything else on your screen.

The oldest trick, in a new place

The attack that caught Comet is not new. It was found within weeks of Auto-GPT’s release.

In June 2023, a researcher named Lukas Euler set up a web page and asked Auto-GPT to summarize it.[2] The page looked blank in places. Those blank spots held text sized to zero pixels, invisible to a person and perfectly legible to a program. The hidden text told the assistant to run a command on the researcher’s computer. It did. Worse, when Auto-GPT paused to show the user what it was about to do, the injected instructions had it print a fake, harmless-looking message so the user would approve. In the researcher’s test setup, the assistant then broke out of the protective container it was running in and reached the host machine.

Two years later, in June 2025, a security firm called Aim Labs showed that Microsoft 365 Copilot could be made to leak a user’s private documents with a single crafted email, which the user never had to open.[4] Microsoft rated the flaw critical and patched it on its own servers before customers had to do anything.[5] Two months after that came Comet.

Three products, three years, the same move. Put instructions where the assistant will read them, and the assistant treats them as yours.

Why it keeps happening

Every piece of software you have ever used draws a line between two kinds of text. There is the code, which the computer obeys, and there is the data, which the computer merely handles. Your email program runs its own code. It does not run the emails.

A language model has no such line. It takes in one long stream of words, your request, the web page, the email, the search result, and treats all of it as material to reason about and act on. The researchers who first named this problem, a team led by Kai Greshake, put it plainly in 2023. These systems “blur the line between data and instructions.”[1] Two years later the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal agency that writes the rulebook on measurement, listed the same attack in its official catalog of threats to AI and gave the same reason.[6]

Think of a new employee who follows every sticky note on their desk, no matter who left it. You can train that employee to be suspicious. You can lock the office door. But you cannot make them stop reading.

That is what makes this different from an ordinary bug. A bug gets fixed. This is a property of how the technology works. Every defense in the research, and I read all the major ones for this story, accepts that the assistant will keep reading strangers’ instructions and tries to limit the damage when it obeys.

Every fix takes something away

The defenses sort into two families. One family tries to teach the model better manners, training it to rank your instructions above anything it reads on a page. OpenAI published this approach under the name “instruction hierarchy” and reported large gains with little loss of ability.[10] The other family does not trust the model at all. It builds walls around it. The assistant may only use tools it chose before reading anything. Or a second, dumber program tracks where every piece of information came from and refuses to let anything from a web page steer a decision.[8]

The research describes each of these costs, when it describes them at all, as a drop in the assistant’s score on a test. It almost never describes them as your time.

The label and the lab

There is a second problem, and it is the one that should change how you read a product announcement.

In 2024, researchers at Microsoft published a defense called spotlighting. The idea is to mark everything the assistant reads from the outside world so the model can tell it apart from your instructions, like printing a visitor’s badge on every page. In their own tests, the attack success rate fell from more than half to “below 2%.”[9]

Later that year, an independent team at ETH Zurich built a test suite called AgentDojo, nearly a hundred realistic tasks for an assistant and more than six hundred planted attacks, and ran the leading defenses through it.[7] The spotlighting idea, applied to OpenAI’s GPT-4o, let 42 attacks in 100 through.

Both numbers are honest. They measure different things, with different attacks, on different setups. But only one of them is the number you will see in a press release, and it is not the one from the outside lab. Anyone who has compared the calorie count on a menu with a nutrition label knows the feeling. When a company tells you its assistant is safe, the useful question is who checked.

The independent test also makes the trade-off visible. On AgentDojo, the defense that blocked the most attacks on GPT-4o, picking tools first, let only 7 in 100 through. The defense that kept the assistant most useful, repeating your instructions after every page it reads, still let 28 in 100 through.[7] Nothing tested was both as safe and as capable as the unprotected assistant. There is no free fix. There is only a choice about who pays.

The cost no one counts

I came to this subject as a graduate student in software engineering who wants to build these assistants, and I spent a semester reading what the people who make them have written about keeping them safe. What struck me was not any single finding. It was the unit of measurement.

Every paper measures a defense the same way, by what the assistant can still do. Tasks completed, out of a hundred. That is a reasonable number for an engineer. It is the wrong number for you. The assistant losing seven tasks in a hundred means seven times you will do the job yourself, and the paper never says which seven, or how long they take, or whether you will know in advance. In the whole body of research, one paper comes close to naming this, with the phrase “user fatigue,” and it does so in passing, on the way to a different point.[8]

So who pays for safety? Right now, you do, and nobody is sending you the bill. The company that chooses the defense knows what it took away. You will find out one pop-up and one stalled task at a time.

What you can do

None of this means you should refuse the help. It means you should accept it with your eyes open. Four things follow from the research.

  1. Do not give one assistant all three keys. The security writer Simon Willison calls them the lethal trifecta. Private data, content from strangers, and a way to send things out.[11] An assistant with all three can be robbed. Let it read your email, or browse the open web, but think hard before you let it do both in the same session.
  2. Turn on “ask before acting,” and treat the pop-ups as the price. If a product offers a setting that pauses before sending or deleting anything, use it. The pop-ups are the cost of the fix, made visible. The day you start clicking yes without reading is the day the fix stops working.
  3. When a company says its assistant is safe, ask who tested it. A number from the maker’s own lab is a label. A number from an outside test is a lab result. They are not the same, and the gap can be forty points.[7] [9]
  4. Watch for what it quietly stops doing. The clearest sign that a product has added a safety fix is a task it used to finish and now hands back to you. That is the cost, arriving. It is worth paying. It is also worth noticing.

The assistant in that Brave experiment did exactly what it was built to do. It read the page it was given and acted on what it found. The hard part was never teaching a computer to follow instructions. The hard part is deciding whose instructions count, and being honest about what we lose each time we narrow the answer.
