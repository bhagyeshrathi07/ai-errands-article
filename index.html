Scams in the Age of AI

Can You Trust What the AI in Your Inbox Tells You?

Gmail now writes a short AI summary at the top of your emails for billions of people. Scammers have found a way to make that summary lie. Here is how the trick works, and a simple rule for when to trust it.

By Bhagyesh RathiSan José State University9 min read

Last July, a security researcher named Marco Figueroa sent himself an email and asked Gmail to summarize it. The email looked ordinary. The summary did not. At the bottom, in the calm voice of Google’s own assistant, it warned him that his Gmail password had been compromised and gave him a phone number to call.[2]

The email had never said that. Figueroa had typed the warning into the message in white letters shrunk to zero size, where no person would ever see them, along with an instruction telling the assistant to repeat it. Gmail’s assistant read the invisible text, treated it as an order, and put a scammer’s words in Google’s mouth.[2] He reported it through a program run by Mozilla that pays researchers for flaws in AI products. Google has published protections against this kind of trick, and no one has reported it being used on real people. The point of the demonstration is how little it took.

If you are thinking you would never fall for that, consider who the warning appears to come from. Not a stranger. Not a misspelled address. A summary your email app wrote for you, in the same place it tells you your package has shipped. That is the whole trick, and it is new. For as long as there have been scams, the scammer had to reach you. Now there is something between you and your mail that reads every word on your behalf, and it can be reached instead.

This is already in your inbox

You do not have to buy anything or sign up for anything for this to apply to you. Gmail has more than three billion accounts, and in June 2026 Google began showing its AI summaries on free accounts around the world, not just paid ones.[12] Microsoft has built its assistant, Copilot, into Outlook.[4] About half of American adults now use an AI chatbot, and a quarter use one every day.[16] The assistant that reads your mail is not a product you will be offered someday. For most people it arrived this year, switched on.

And you were already worried. In a Pew Research Center survey of more than nine thousand adults, 73 percent said they had been hit by at least one online scam or attack, nearly two thirds said they get scam emails at least once a week, and 68 percent expected AI to make scams more common.[15] They were right, but not quite in the way they feared. The fear was fake voices and better-written phishing. The new way in is quieter. The scammer no longer has to fool you. They have to fool the assistant you trust to read for you.

So the question I set out to answer is the one you probably already have. Can you trust the AI that reads your mail, and how would you know? I spent a semester reading what the people who build and test these systems have published. The honest answer is yes, for the things it can only tell you, as long as you keep one habit. Not yet, for the things it is about to start doing for you. The rest of this article explains why, and ends with a rule you can use.

Why the assistant falls for it

Every piece of software you have ever used draws a line between two kinds of text. There is the program, which the computer obeys, and there is the data, which the computer merely handles. Your email app runs its own code. It does not run your emails.

The AI behind these assistants has no such line. It takes in one long stream of words, your request, the email, the web page, the search result, and treats all of it as material to act on. The researchers who first described this problem, a team led by Kai Greshake, put it plainly in 2023. These systems “blur the line between data and instructions.”[1] Two years later the National Institute of Standards and Technology, the federal agency that writes the rulebook on measurement, listed the same trick in its official catalog of threats to AI and gave the same reason.[6]

Think of a new employee who follows every sticky note on their desk, no matter who left it. You can train that employee to be suspicious. You can lock the office door. But you cannot make them stop reading. That is why this is not an ordinary bug. A bug gets fixed. This is a property of how the technology works, and every defense in the research accepts it and tries to limit the damage. Security researchers have a technical name for the trick. This article calls it what it is, a hidden note the assistant obeys.

Today it reads. Next, it acts.

Here is the good news about the Gmail trick. A summary can only tell you something. You still have to pick up the phone, and if you do not, nothing is lost. The assistant reads for you. It does not yet act for you.

That is the part the companies want to change. In October 2025, OpenAI released its own web browser with a mode that opens tabs and clicks on your behalf.[13] Google has said the same abilities are coming to Chrome, the browser most people already use, and showed one filling a grocery cart from a list in an email.[14] Microsoft’s Copilot can already read your documents and send mail.[4] The pitch is a good one. Ask it to find a dentist that takes your insurance and book the earliest slot, and it will browse, compare and click submit. Most of what makes a computer tedious is the clicking, and here is a program offering to do the clicking.

And when the assistant can act, the scammer no longer needs you to do anything at all. A month before the Gmail demonstration, a security firm called Aim Labs showed that Microsoft’s Copilot could be made to leak a person’s private documents with a single crafted email the person never opened.[4] Microsoft rated the flaw critical and fixed it on its own computers.[5] A month after the Gmail demonstration, researchers at Brave, a browser company, asked a rival browser’s assistant to summarize a Reddit thread. A comment hidden in the thread told it to open the user’s email, find a one-time login code and post it where the attacker could read it. It did. The user clicked nothing.[3]

The hidden note in Figueroa’s email asked the assistant to say something. The one in the Reddit thread asked it to do something, and it did that too. Same trick, three products, one summer. So the natural question is whether the companies have fixed it.

Do the protections work?

Partly. The companies have published real protections, and they help. One approach trains the model to ignore instructions that show up inside an email or a web page.[10] Another marks everything the assistant reads from the outside world so it can tell those words apart from yours.[9] A third does not trust the model at all and lets a second program block anything it was talked into.[8]

An independent test shows how far they go. In 2024 a university team in Switzerland built nearly a hundred realistic errands for an assistant, planted more than six hundred hidden notes in them, and ran the leading protections through the course on one of OpenAI’s main models.[7] With no protection, 58 attacks in 100 succeeded. The best protection tested cut that to 7. Most of the others still let 28 or more through. Not one stopped every note.

Seven in a hundred is a big improvement. It is also not a number you would accept from a lock on your front door. So the question for you is not whether the assistant is protected. It is what the assistant can reach if a note gets through, and whether a company’s safety claim is the number to believe.

The label and the lab

Here is the second thing to know before you trust a company that says its assistant is protected.

In 2024, researchers at Microsoft published a fix. The idea is to mark everything the assistant reads from the outside world so it can tell those words apart from your instructions, like printing a visitor’s badge on every page. In Microsoft’s own tests, the share of attacks that worked fell from more than half to “below 2%.”[9] On the independent Swiss test, the same idea let 42 attacks in 100 through.[7]

Both numbers are honest. They measure different attacks on different setups. But only one of them is the number you will see in a press release, and it is not the one from the outside lab. Anyone who has compared the calorie count on a menu with the nutrition label on the box knows the feeling. When a company tells you its assistant is protected, the useful question is who checked.

So, can you trust it?

Trust it to read. Do not yet trust it to act on its own.

The line runs through what the assistant can reach. A security writer named Simon Willison boiled it down to three things an assistant must hold at once before it can rob you. Your private data, content from strangers, and a way to send things out.[11] An assistant with all three can be robbed. An assistant with two of them cannot. The Gmail summary held your mail and a stranger’s email, but it had no way to send anything out. All it could do was lie to you. The browser assistant could read your email, read a Reddit page, and post. Copilot could read your documents, read an email, and send. Those two were robbed.

That gives you a rule you can actually use, and it sorts the things an assistant can do for you into three piles.

Four habits

  1. Treat urgency in a summary the way you treat urgency in a text from an unknown number. A real warning from Google does not arrive inside the summary of someone else’s email. Open the actual message, and if there is a number to call, look it up yourself.[2]
  2. If you would rather not have the summaries, turn them off. In Gmail, open Settings, then General, and switch off the Workspace smart features. You can still ask for a summary by hand when you want one.[12]
  3. Count the keys before you let it act. Your private data, strangers’ content, a way to send things out. Let the assistant have two. Never all three in one session.[11]
  4. When a company says it is protected, ask who tested it. A number from the maker’s own lab is a label. A number from an outside test is a lab result. The gap can be forty points.[7] [9]

The assistant in Figueroa’s inbox did exactly what it was built to do. It read what it was given and repeated what it found. The hard part was never teaching a computer to follow instructions. The hard part is teaching it whose instructions count, and nobody has finished that job. Until they do, the rule is short. Let it read. Do not let it act alone.
